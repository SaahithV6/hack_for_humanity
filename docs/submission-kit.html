Part two

Devpost copy

Paste straight into the submission form. Written to be read by a judge who has thirty of these to get through.

Inspiration

Every one of us has lain in bed at 1am, not enjoying the feed, and not able to put it down. The framing we kept hearing — that this is a willpower problem — didn't match the experience at all.

What actually seems to happen is a loop. Stress makes the feed attractive, the feed delivers unpredictable small rewards, and the reward makes the stress worse. Screen-time blockers attack the last link in that chain and leave the first one untouched, which is why everybody uninstalls them within a week.

The loop also runs at the worst possible hour. Going to bed means sitting with an unnarrated day, and an unnarrated day defaults to I did nothing today. So you keep scrolling to avoid arriving at that thought. We wanted to build the thing that gives you the dopamine somewhere else, and then narrates the day so bed stops being the place you're avoiding.

What it does

Moth is a small on-device buddy that catches you mid-scroll and lands you before bed.

  • Six questions. A PHQ-2-style screen and a bedtime. That's the whole setup.
  • One task at a time. Never a list — a list lets you scan, compare and pick nothing, which is the same paralysis the feed causes, and a list of undone tasks is a list of small failures.
  • "I'm stuck scrolling." One button hands back something you can start in ten seconds.
  • It learns. Moth works out which kinds of things you actually do, at which times and at what energy, and stops offering the rest.
  • You take over. After five of its tasks, you start writing your own — and it predicts them, so it costs a few taps rather than a paragraph at midnight.
  • At bedtime it interrupts you and reads the day back using things that actually happened. Then it says goodnight, and there is nothing else in the app to look at.

It is grounded in behavioural activation, an established treatment for depression that inverts the usual intuition: you don't wait until you feel like it — the action comes first and the motivation follows. Every part of the app maps onto a piece of that protocol, including handing the scheduling back to the person, which is why the app is designed to get out of your way.

How we built it

Native SwiftUI, no third-party dependencies at all — you open the project and hit Run.

The generator is not a wrapper around an API. It's a small model built to run in a few megabytes on a 4GB phone, offline:

  • A context-conditioned probabilistic grammar — 45 frames and 12 slots, each gated on energy, mood, time of day and minutes-to-bedtime. "Step outside and look at the sky" is a good suggestion at 6pm and a bad one at 1am, and the grammar knows the difference.
  • Thompson sampling over seven activity domains, with separate Beta posteriors per time-of-day × energy bucket and a daily decay, so who you were three months ago stops outvoting who you are now. We learn this rather than asking, because depressed people routinely mispredict what will help them.
  • An asymmetric difficulty ladder that climbs slowly and drops fast. Getting difficulty wrong downward costs one easy task; getting it wrong upward costs the user's belief that the app works.
  • An order-3 backoff n-gram trained on the shipped corpus and on your own writing, which is what turns writing a task into three taps.

One optional cloud model writes the bedtime summary, because that's the single place a language model beats us — and everything it returns is re-validated on the device before a word reaches the screen. Every number it states must be one we supplied; every quotation must be a task that really happened. The model only ever writes prose: the counts, minutes, streak and highlights are always the local engine's.

The engine is plain Swift with no Apple-framework imports, so it builds and tests on Linux as well as in the app — which is how most of it got written and tested without a Mac in the room.

Challenges we ran into

Two holes in our guardrails that only a live model found. We built the validator against handwritten adversarial fixtures and it passed everything. Then we pointed it at a real model and watched it write "four things in thirteen minutes" — spelled out. Our anti-hallucination check only scanned digits, so an invented count would have sailed straight through. In the other direction, it was rejecting correct output for saying "five" when the real task was "Name five things you can see" — punishing the model for quoting us accurately. Fixing both took live acceptance from 6/8 to 10/10.

Effort ceilings that didn't actually bind. A test caught the engine offering a fifteen-minute task to someone forty minutes from bed. The ladder's target was a soft preference the grammar was free to drift past — fine most of the time, wrong exactly when it mattered.

Sentences that fell apart in the middle. Repeated slots in one frame were sampling independently, producing "Clear your bag. Only your nightstand." Slots now bind once per sentence.

Writing an iOS app from Linux. No Mac, so no compiler for the UI layer. We pushed everything we could into a platform-free Swift package that builds and tests locally, kept the SwiftUI layer thin, and syntax-checked it with swiftc -parse — which caught six invalid string literals before they ever reached a teammate's machine.

Accomplishments that we're proud of

It works with the network off. Not "we promise not to send your data" — the whole product functions in airplane mode, on a 4GB phone, because the generator is ours and it's tiny.

We used a language model in exactly one place, and we don't trust it. 31 adversarial tests fire realistic bad output at the validator — invented accomplishments, fabricated counts, "amazing work today", bedtime advice, a suggestion to go scroll Instagram — and each is caught for the right reason. The app shows you its own accept and reject counts, because a guardrail nobody can see isn't a guardrail.

The safety gate outranks the product. If the intake screen or any text field indicates crisis, the app stops being about streaks and puts three phone numbers on the screen. Gamifying someone in distress would be actively harmful, so that path bypasses the engine entirely.

A zero day is not a failure state. The summary for a day where nothing got done is written with as much care as any other, because "you completed 0 of 4 tasks" is precisely the input that starts the next doomscroll.

59 tests, all passing, including regressions for tone and output quality — not just correctness.

What we learned

Test guardrails against the thing they guard, not against your imagination of it. Our validator was perfect against fixtures and had two real holes the moment a live model touched it. Adversarial tests written by the same person who wrote the code share that person's blind spots.

Knowing where not to use a model is the design work. A language model is worse than our bandit at choosing what to offer, worse than a constant at setting difficulty, and dangerous at crisis screening. It's better at exactly one thing — writing warmly — so that's the only job it has.

Tone is a technical requirement. For this user, at this hour, "Amazing work today!" makes every other thing the app says untrustworthy. That's not copywriting polish; it's a constraint the validator enforces in code.

What's next for Moth

Learn the scroll, not just the tasks. Screen Time's DeviceActivity API can tell us when a session is running long without telling us what's in it, so Moth could offer the rescue before you think to ask.

Swap the cloud model for an on-device one. Apple's Foundation Models framework would let the summary be written locally on newer hardware, with the heuristic engine still covering everything older. The validator already sits behind a protocol, so the seam exists.

Widen the corpus and localise the crisis resources, which are US-only today.

Actually test the hypothesis. Everything here rests on the claim that a narrated day makes bed easier to arrive at. We believe it, and we'd like to find out — bedtime drift over a few weeks is measurable entirely on-device.
